## The Question Everyone Asks Before Buying a Ticket

Do lucky numbers actually win the lottery?

The obvious answer, the one any statistician will hand you before you finish the sentence, is no. The odds of winning the Powerball jackpot are 1 in 292,201,338, and those odds do not care whether you picked your grandmother's birthday, the numbers from a fortune cookie you cracked open at a Panda Express in Sacramento, or a sequence generated by a quick-pick machine spitting out thermal-printed digits at your local 7-Eleven. Each combination has exactly the same infinitesimal chance. The Multi-State Lottery Association has confirmed this repeatedly. Math doesn't bend for sentiment.

So on the raw question of "will my lucky numbers make me more likely to win," the answer is: no, they will not. If you came here looking for permission to believe otherwise on strictly mathematical grounds, I can't give it to you.

But that's not actually the whole story, and if it were, this would be a very short post. Because "do lucky numbers work" turns out to be a more interesting question than it looks. There's a version of the answer where lucky numbers matter enormously — just not in the way most players think.

## Why the Obvious Answer Is Incomplete

Here's what the "it's just math, dummy" crowd misses: lottery outcomes aren't only about probability of winning. They're also about what happens *if* you win, and what happens *while* you're playing, and why you're playing in the first place.

Consider the split-the-jackpot problem. In a 2010 study published in the Journal of Gambling Studies, researchers found that 62% of lottery players use personally meaningful numbers — birthdays, anniversaries, the ages of their kids. Which means the numbers 1 through 31 are dramatically overselected across every drawing, every week, in every state. Joseph Mazur, the mathematician who wrote *What's Luck Got to Do with It?* in 2010, walked through the arithmetic clearly: if you pick numbers only from 1-31, you don't lower your odds of winning, but you significantly raise your odds of *sharing* the prize with dozens of other people who did the same thing.

That's a genuine, mathematically real consequence of using your kid's birthday. Your "lucky" numbers may cost you millions in a split — not because they're unlucky, but because they're *everyone else's* lucky numbers too.

Then there's the psychological layer, which is where things get genuinely strange. The act of playing a lottery isn't purely economic. It's ritual. It's hope. It's the two-minute daydream on the drive home about what you'd do with the money. And rituals, as anyone who's ever kept a rabbit's foot or worn the same socks to a job interview knows, do real work on your mood, your confidence, and your willingness to keep showing up. Dismissing all of that as irrational leaves out most of what actually happens when a person buys a ticket.

So the honest answer requires going deeper than the odds.

## The Real Answer: What's Actually Happening When You Pick Lucky Numbers

Start with a pigeon.

In 1948, B.F. Skinner ran an experiment at Indiana University that would end up in every intro psychology textbook. He put hungry pigeons in boxes and delivered food pellets at random intervals — completely unlinked to anything the birds did. Within days, the pigeons developed elaborate rituals. One kept turning counterclockwise between feedings. Another bobbed its head toward a specific corner. A third swung its body in a pendulum motion. Each pigeon had, in effect, invented a superstition. Whatever it happened to be doing when the food arrived became the thing it kept doing, hoping to make the food arrive again.

Skinner's point wasn't really about pigeons. It was about us. Human beings are pattern-recognition machines running on a brain that evolved to spot cause and effect even when there isn't any. When you picked the numbers 7, 14, and 22 for a scratcher and won $40, some part of your brain quietly filed those digits away as *meaningful*. Play them again next week and you're doing exactly what Skinner's pigeon was doing, just with better vocabulary.

Now layer on what psychologist Paul Meehl described in 1956 when he coined the term "Barnum effect" — the tendency to accept vague, general statements as uniquely, personally true. "Your lucky number is 8" feels revelatory in the same way a horoscope feels revelatory. It's a hook the mind is hungry to bite. Meehl was writing about personality assessments, but the mechanism transfers cleanly to lottery play. The number feels *yours* the moment someone tells you it is.

None of this makes the belief silly. It makes it human. And here's where the story gets genuinely interesting: believing in luck may actually change your behavior in ways that matter — for better and worse.

In 2003, psychologists Shane Darke and Jonathan Freedman published research in Personality and Individual Differences showing that people who strongly believed in personal luck were more persistent gamblers. They kept playing longer, kept trying, kept showing up. On the one hand, in a gambling context, that's the exact behavior casinos and lotteries are engineered to exploit — persistence is how the house wins. On the other hand, that same trait, that "I'm the kind of person good things happen to" orientation, correlates with people who take more chances in life generally, apply for more jobs, ask more people out, start more businesses. Luck belief is a double-edged tool.

Which brings us to the cultural dimension, the part that pure probability can never touch.

On August 8, 2008, at exactly 8:08:08 PM, the opening ceremony of the Beijing Olympics began. This was not an accident. In Mandarin, the number 8 is pronounced *bā*, which sounds close to *fā* (发), the word associated with wealth and prosperity. For roughly a fifth of humanity, the number 8 is not a superstition — it's a piece of infrastructure. Phone numbers with lots of 8s sell at premium prices in Hong Kong. License plates get auctioned. Wedding dates get chosen. When a Chinese lottery player picks 8, they're not being irrational; they're participating in a cultural logic that has organized decisions for generations.

That's a useful frame for the whole lucky-numbers question. Lucky numbers aren't magic. They're meaning. They're the way a person imports their history and their culture and their memory of the day their daughter was born into a piece of paper with a printed grid on it.

Which is why the $2.04 billion Powerball winner from November 7, 2022 — the largest jackpot in history — is such an interesting case. The winning numbers were 10, 33, 41, 47, 56, and Powerball 10. The winner has never said publicly whether these were considered lucky, or were a quick-pick, or came from a dream, or a fortune slip, or their address in Altadena. And the funny thing is: it doesn't matter. Once someone wins, whatever numbers they used *become* lucky in retrospect. That's how the whole belief system sustains itself. Winners tell stories. Losers stop talking.

So do lucky numbers "work"? They work at doing what numbers do when we invest them with meaning. They make us play. They give the ritual weight. They anchor us to people we love, to places we've been, to the version of ourselves who believes something good might still be coming. If you want to check today's [lucky numbers](/lucky-numbers) before buying a ticket, do it — just do it knowing what you're actually doing, which is participating in a very old, very human practice of asking the universe to notice you.

What lucky numbers cannot do is bend a probability distribution. The 1-in-292-million wall is real. But almost nothing anyone does in a given week is about beating probability distributions. Most of what we do is about making meaning inside them.

## The Honest Conclusion

Here's what I'd tell a friend who asked me this over coffee: your lucky numbers won't help you win, but they won't hurt you either — with one exception. If you're playing birthdays and anniversaries, you're picking from a pool of 1 through 31 that half the country is also picking from, and if lightning does strike, you may be splitting the pot with fifty other people who used their kids' birthdays too. If a $500 million jackpot matters to you at full size, throw at least one number above 31 into your ticket. That's a mathematically real edge, not on winning, but on winning *alone*.

Beyond that, play the numbers that feel like yours. Pick the ones from your grandmother's birthday, from the fortune you got at that dim sum place in San Francisco, from the jersey number of the player you loved as a kid. Believe in them a little. Ritual is not a bug in human cognition — it's a feature, and it's part of why playing feels worth the two dollars in the first place. Just don't confuse the meaning for the mechanism. And if you want to [break a fortune cookie](/) before you buy your next ticket, I won't stop you. Skinner's pigeons would understand completely.
