Superstitions get a bad rap. But the same rituals your grandmother swore by — the lucky socks, the knocked wood, the crossed fingers — turn out to be surprisingly effective cognitive tools, and the research backing them is more serious than you'd expect. Here are seven superstition-adjacent techniques that measurably sharpen decision-making, each with the study that proves it and something concrete to try before the week is out.

## 1. Bring a Physical Lucky Object Into High-Stakes Rooms

Put something you've assigned meaning to on the desk, the podium, the counter — and watch your performance climb.

In 2010, psychologist Lysann Damisch ran a now-famous experiment at the University of Cologne. She let participants bring a personal lucky charm into a memory and anagram test. The charm-holders solved an average of 6.4 anagrams out of 15. The control group, charmless, managed only 4.4. That's not noise — that's a 45% improvement from a keychain. In a companion study published in Psychological Science, golfers handed a ball marked "LUCKY" in felt-tip pen sank 6.4 putts out of 10, compared with 4.75 for golfers given an ordinary ball. Same ball. Same green. Different belief.

Damisch's interpretation, backed by follow-up work, is that the charm raises self-efficacy — the private conviction that you can pull this off — which in turn drives you to try harder and give up later.

**Try this today:** Pick one object. A coin, a smooth stone from a beach you love, your grandfather's watch. Carry it in your pocket to the next meeting, interview, or difficult conversation where you want to be sharper. Touch it once before you begin. You've just borrowed a 15-point confidence boost backed by peer review.

## 2. Build a Pre-Decision Ritual (Even a Weird One)

Before a hard choice, do the same small sequence of actions every time. The weirder and more private, the better.

A 2016 study out of the University of Chicago Booth School of Business tracked people performing high-pressure tasks like public singing and timed math. Participants who performed a brief ritual beforehand — a specific sequence of movements, counts, or gestures — improved their performance by up to 15% and reported markedly less anxiety. The ritual itself didn't need to be meaningful. It just needed to be consistent.

Jane Risen, the behavioral scientist at Chicago Booth who's spent her career on magical thinking, has shown that even when you *know* a ritual is irrational, the emotional benefit survives intact. Your prefrontal cortex can shrug and your gut will still relax.

**Try this today:** Design a 20-second pre-decision ritual. Three deep breaths, two taps on the table, one sip of water. Or something more personal — recite a line from a song, trace a letter on your palm. Use it before you send the email you've been drafting for three days. Use it before you say yes or no to the job offer. The specific actions don't matter; the repetition does. Within two weeks you'll notice the ritual itself starts to signal "I'm ready to decide."

## 3. Use the "Fingers Crossed" Intervention on Someone Else

When a friend is about to attempt something hard, tell them explicitly that you're wishing them luck — out loud, with the words.

Damisch's Cologne lab tested the German phrase *"Ich drücke dir die Daumen"* — literally "I press the thumb for you," the German equivalent of fingers crossed. Participants who heard this phrase before attempting an impossible puzzle persisted an average of 24% longer than those who received a neutral send-off. They didn't solve more puzzles (the puzzles were unsolvable), but they fought harder and quit later. In the real world, where most problems *are* solvable with enough persistence, that extra 24% is the entire ballgame.

Stuart Vyse, whose 1997 book *Believing in Magic: The Psychology of Superstition* remains the canonical text, calls this the social activation of self-efficacy. Being wished luck isn't magical thinking — it's a verbal cue that someone believes in your outcome, and belief is contagious.

**Try this week:** When your partner has a nerve-wracking presentation, don't say "you'll do great." Say "I'm crossing my fingers for you right now" and show them your crossed fingers. When your kid has a test, text them a photo of your hand. Specificity matters. The gesture plus the words plus the proof — that's the full dose.

## 4. Match Your Ritual Intensity to Your Uncertainty

The more unpredictable the outcome, the more elaborate your ritual should be. Save the heavy magic for the genuinely uncertain stuff.

Anthropologist George Gmelch spent years studying baseball players and noticed something elegant: pitchers and batters, who succeed only about 30% of the time, develop baroque superstitions. Fielders, who catch roughly 97% of what comes their way, have almost none. Gmelch's most famous subject was Turk Wendell, the Mets reliever who brushed his teeth between every inning and chewed exactly four pieces of black licorice while on the mound. Wendell wasn't crazy. He was pitching, which is to say he was failing seven times out of ten even on good days, and the licorice was a tool for staying functional inside that failure rate.

The lesson scales. For routine, predictable decisions — what to eat for lunch, which route to drive — rituals are wasted overhead. For genuinely uncertain decisions — a career pivot, a diagnosis to interpret, a bet on a person — a stronger ritual earns its keep.

**Try this today:** Rank your current open decisions by how uncertain the outcome truly is. For anything above 50% uncertainty, build a ritual. For anything below, skip it. If you want a quick, low-cost ritual for the big-uncertainty stuff, [break a fortune cookie](/) before you decide — the tiny ceremony is the point.

## 5. Lean on Superstition When Anxiety Is Highest

Counter-intuitive but well-documented: ritualistic thinking is most useful precisely when your environment feels most out of control.

Giora Keinan's 1994 research during the Gulf War tracked Israeli citizens in Tel Aviv as Scud missiles fell. He found that people living in high-threat neighborhoods developed significantly more superstitious behaviors — specific touching rituals, lucky objects placed by the door, patterned routines before leaving the apartment — than people in lower-threat areas. More striking: those who adopted these rituals reported lower anxiety and functioned better day-to-day. The superstition wasn't a symptom. It was a coping technology.

This is the mechanism Vyse calls "perceived control." You cannot stop a missile. You *can* touch the doorframe three times on your way out. The brain, offered a lever, pulls it, and the pull itself calms the system enough to let you make better small decisions all day long.

**Try this week:** Identify the area of your life where you currently feel the least control — a parent's health, a layoff rumor, a stalled apartment search. Attach one small ritual to it. Light a candle each morning before you check the news. Draw a card from a deck. Check your [daily fortune](/daily). Not to change the outcome — to change how you move through the waiting.

## 6. Treat Pre-Performance Routines as Non-Negotiable Infrastructure

If 80% of elite athletes do something, there's probably a reason — and there's no reason the technique should stay locked inside sports.

A 2010 study in the *Journal of Applied Sport Psychology* surveyed elite athletes across disciplines and found that 80% engaged in pre-performance routines containing superstitious elements. Not the top 80% — 80% of the elite tier. These weren't fringe believers. They were Olympians and professionals who had every incentive to drop ineffective habits and hadn't.

The routines did two things simultaneously. First, they narrowed attention: by executing a known sequence, the athlete offloaded working memory and freed up cognitive bandwidth for the actual performance. Second, they created a psychological threshold — a before-and-after line that signaled "the thing starts now."

**Try this today:** Build a 60-second routine you perform before any work session that matters. Clear the desk. Pour the same drink into the same cup. Open the document. Take three breaths. Begin. Do this for ten consecutive work sessions and you'll notice the routine itself starts triggering focus the way a bell triggered Pavlov's dogs. You've built a cognitive on-switch.

## 7. Consult an External Oracle to Surface What You Already Think

When you can't decide, flip a coin — not to obey it, but to notice how you feel the instant it lands.

This technique has no single study behind it, but it draws on decades of work by Jane Risen and others showing that intuitive "gut" responses carry genuine information the analytical mind suppresses. The flipped coin, the tarot card, the fortune cookie slip — these aren't predictions. They're mirrors. The moment the result appears, your body telegraphs preference: relief, disappointment, resistance. That reaction is data you couldn't access through deliberation alone, because deliberation keeps negotiating with itself. The oracle short-circuits the negotiation.

Risen's work shows that even sophisticated, educated people experience this gut reaction when confronted with a random outcome, and the reaction tends to be more honest than their stated preferences.

**Try this today:** Pick a decision you've been circling for more than a week. Assign the two options to heads and tails. Flip. The instant it lands, check your gut before your head. Or draw [lucky numbers](/lucky-numbers) and notice which option they seem to favor and whether you're pleased or annoyed. The oracle doesn't decide. It extracts your decision from wherever you'd buried it.

## The Pattern Underneath

What links these seven techniques isn't magic. It's that each one introduces a structured pause between stimulus and response, and that pause is where good decisions actually happen. The lucky charm, the muttered ritual, the crossed fingers, the flipped coin — they're all small ceremonies that interrupt your default reactivity and insert a moment of chosen behavior. Damisch's charms, Wendell's licorice, Keinan's doorframe-tappers in Tel Aviv — all of them were buying themselves a sliver of agency inside circumstances that offered very little.

You don't have to believe in luck to use luck's tools. You just have to notice that the brain, offered a ritual, calms down enough to think clearly, and that clarity is worth more than any charm. Maybe the fortune in the cookie is right. Maybe it isn't. Either way, the pause while you crack it open is already working.
