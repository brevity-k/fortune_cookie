## The Weight of a Book Falling Open

In a quiet corner of the Aramgah-e Hafez in Shiraz, a woman in her sixties presses her palm against the cover of a leather-bound book. The leather is tooled with vines and worn smooth where thousands of other palms have rested before hers. Gold leaf flakes from the spine. The air smells of rosewater and dust and the faint green note of cypress trees from the garden outside. She closes her eyes, whispers a question she has not spoken aloud to anyone, and lets the Divan of Hafez fall open in her hands.

She does not choose a page. The book chooses.

Her thumb lands on a ghazal somewhere in the middle — one of 486 lyric poems the fourteenth-century Persian poet composed before his death in 1390. A young man beside her, perhaps her grandson, reads the lines aloud in soft Farsi. She listens. Her face changes. Whatever Hafez has said to her across six hundred years, it is enough.

This is bibliomancy — the ancient art of asking a book a question and trusting the book to answer. It is one of the oldest forms of fortune-telling humans have ever practiced, and it survives, right now, this morning, in a marble mausoleum in southern Iran where pilgrims line up with questions about marriages, illnesses, and the shape of years to come.

## The Emperor Who Asked Virgil

The practice has a long memory. Around 130 CE, the Roman Emperor Hadrian — then still consolidating his hold on an empire that stretched from the Scottish borders to the Euphrates — walked into the Temple of Zeus at Heliopolis in what is now Baalbek, Lebanon. He carried with him a scroll of Virgil's Aeneid, the epic poem the Roman poet had completed just before his death in 19 BCE.

Hadrian was practicing the Sortes Virgilianae, the Virgilian Lots. The ritual was simple and audacious: formulate a question in your mind, open the scroll at random, and read whatever your eyes fell upon as a message from the fates. According to Aelius Spartianus, writing in the Historia Augusta, Hadrian's eyes fell on Book VI, lines 808 to 812 — a passage describing a figure of great authority and destiny. He read it as a confirmation of his succession to the imperial throne, and from that moment on, he treated Virgil's text with the reverence usually reserved for prophetic scripture.

What makes this strange and marvelous is that Virgil had been dead for 150 years. He was a poet, not an oracle. He never claimed divine inspiration. And yet an emperor who commanded legions chose to put a serious question about his future to a book of poetry, and chose to live by the answer.

The Romans did this constantly. Soldiers before battle. Senators before votes. Lovers before weddings. The Aeneid became what we might call Rome's unofficial oracle — a 9,896-line poem that doubled as a divination tool for anyone literate enough to unroll a scroll.

## The Church That Could Not Stop It

When Christianity swept through Europe, the practice did not die — it simply changed books. Monks and parishioners began opening the Bible at random, a practice called Sortes Sanctorum, or Holy Lots. The Church, uneasy about its flock treating scripture like a magic eight-ball, moved to shut it down.

The Council of Vannes in 465 CE issued one of the earliest official condemnations. Forty-one years later, the Council of Agde in 506 CE repeated the prohibition in sharper language. Priests who practiced it faced defrocking. Laypeople faced penance.

It did not work. Medieval sortes manuscripts preserved in collections like the Eton College Library — some dating to the thirteenth century, their vellum pages roughly 8 by 11 inches, slightly waxy to the touch, carrying that faint musky scent that old animal skin never loses — show the evidence plainly. Small marks appear beside certain lines. Fingerprints darken the edges of particular passages. Whole gatherings fall open naturally at the same spots, the spine broken in by centuries of being asked the same kinds of questions.

Monks did it in their cells. Kings did it in libraries. One of the most famous instances comes from 1643, when King Charles I of England reportedly visited the Bodleian Library in Oxford and opened a Bible at random. The passage he found seemed, to him and to his courtiers, to foretell catastrophe. Six years later, in January 1649, he was beheaded in Whitehall. Whether the Bible actually predicted his death or whether a frightened king read his fears into whatever he found — that depends on what you believe about books and the people who hold them.

## Yarrow Stalks and the Book of Changes

Half a world away, in the courts of the Western Zhou dynasty around 1000 BCE, a different kind of bibliomancy was already ancient. The I Ching — the Book of Changes — is less a book you open than a book you build. Its 64 hexagrams, each composed of six lines that can be either broken or unbroken, generate 4,096 possible transformations between them. To consult it, the practitioner traditionally casts yarrow stalks: dried stems roughly 12 to 18 inches long, pale cream in color, cool to the touch, making a soft clicking sound as they are counted and separated into piles.

The process takes time. A full consultation using the yarrow method can require forty-five minutes of careful counting and division. The point is not speed. The point is that by the time you have cast the stalks and drawn your hexagram, you have been sitting with your question for the better part of an hour. The book answers, yes. But the ritual has already done most of the work.

I have watched a practitioner in Taipei perform this ritual at a small wooden table, the stalks arranged in careful piles, the only sound the rustle of them against the lacquered surface and the ticking of a clock on the wall. When the hexagram was finally drawn and the passage read, the woman asking the question — about whether to leave her job — did not seem surprised by the answer. She seemed, instead, to have already known it, and to be grateful to the book for saying it back to her.

## Dickens, Rabelais, and the Writers Who Asked

Charles Dickens kept a personal library at Gad's Hill Place in Kent that, by the time of his death in 1870, contained books with pages worn in telling patterns. Biographers have noted the evidence suggests Dickens practiced bibliomancy with his own works — opening his own novels at random, asking them questions, listening for what his earlier self might tell his later self.

François Rabelais took the practice even further. In Gargantua and Pantagruel, published in installments between 1532 and 1564, he describes what he calls the Virgilian lottery — a method of stichomancy using three dice to determine, in sequence, the book, the chapter, and the exact line to be consulted. Rabelais was writing satire, but he was also writing from inside a culture where educated people genuinely did this. The joke worked because the practice was real.

## Why We Still Ask Books

What all of this points to is something stubborn in human beings. We carry questions we cannot answer with ordinary thinking. Should I marry this person. Should I leave this city. Is my mother going to recover. Is this grief ever going to end. These questions do not respond to spreadsheets.

A book, opened at random, creates a space where the usual noise stops. You have formulated the question clearly enough to ask it. You have surrendered the answer to something outside yourself — the weight and balance of a binding, the way a spine falls open along its most-used creases. And then you read what is in front of you, and you watch what your own mind does with it.

The psychologist Carl Jung, who wrote the foreword to the Wilhelm translation of the I Ching in 1949, understood this. The book does not predict the future. The book gives you a mirror angled in a way you could not have angled it yourself. What you see in the mirror — that is yours. It was always yours. You just needed the book to help you look.

Which is why bibliomancy survives. In Shiraz, in Oxford, in the Taipei apartments where I Ching is still consulted on Sunday afternoons, in the quiet moment when someone pulls a worn paperback off a shelf and lets it fall open to see what it says — the practice continues because the need continues. You can even [break a fortune cookie](/) and ask it the same kind of question. The vessel changes. The gesture does not.

## Back to the Garden in Shiraz

The woman in the mausoleum closes the Divan of Hafez. She hands it back to the keeper, who sets it carefully on its cushion for the next pilgrim. The gold leaf on the cover catches the morning light. Her grandson helps her up from the bench. They walk out through the cypress garden, past the fountain, past the other pilgrims waiting with their own folded questions.

Whatever Hafez said to her, she carries it now. It will shape the decision she came here to make. Six hundred years after the poet's death, in a country he would barely recognize, a book has done what books have always done — it has given someone back to themselves, one random page at a time.
