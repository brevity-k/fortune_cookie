Do bad fortunes actually come true?

Short answer: no. A slip of paper baked into a wafer cookie in a factory in Brooklyn or Los Angeles does not have the metaphysical horsepower to reroute your week, sour your marriage, or lose you a job. The fortunes are printed in batches — usually red or black ink on strips roughly 3 inches by half an inch — and stuffed into cookies by machines that fold each one during a two-second window while the wafer is still hot and pliable. Nobody at the factory knows you. The message that told you to "beware of a stranger in blue" was almost certainly sitting in a cardboard box next to 40,000 identical copies before it ever met your kung pao chicken.

So that's the honest, obvious answer, and if you came here for it, you have it. But you probably already suspected as much, and you're still here. Which means the real question isn't whether the paper has power. It's whether the *belief* has power — whether reading "You will face difficulty this month" changes something in the way you move through the next thirty days. That question has a much more interesting answer, and it involves a UCLA classroom in 1948, a Harvard psychologist who messed with people's sense of control, and a Nobel laureate who spent a career explaining why your brain is doing something sneaky right now.

## Why "it's just paper" isn't the whole story

Here's the thing about dismissing fortune cookies as random noise: your brain isn't a neutral receiver. It's an aggressive pattern-matcher that will happily grab a vague sentence and weld it to whatever is happening in your life.

Consider what happens the moment you crack the cookie. There's the crisp snap — that specific brittle sound that anyone who's eaten at an American Chinese restaurant knows instantly. Then a half-second of anticipation while you unfold the slip. Then the message. In that little ritual, your attention is at maximum. You're primed. Whatever the paper says gets stamped onto the emotional mood of the meal, the conversation you were having, the argument you had that afternoon.

A negative fortune — "A difficult decision approaches" or "Not everyone is your friend" — lands harder than a positive one for a specific neurological reason. Daniel Kahneman spent decades documenting this in the work that eventually filled his 2011 book *Thinking, Fast and Slow*: humans systematically overweight vivid, emotionally charged information. A threat feels more probable than a mild good outcome, even when the base rates are identical. So when you get a bad fortune, it doesn't just register — it *sticks*. You'll remember it tomorrow. You'll remember it next Tuesday when your car makes a weird noise. And that's where the trouble starts, because the sticking is what makes the fortune seem to "come true," even when nothing unusual happened at all.

## The real answer: what actually happens after you read a bad fortune

Let's start in a classroom at UCLA in 1948. A psychologist named Bertram R. Forer handed his students what he told them was a personalized personality assessment. Each student got a written profile supposedly derived from a test they'd taken the week before. They rated the accuracy of their profile: on average, 4.26 out of 5. Some rated it a perfect 5. Then Forer told them the truth. Every student had received the exact same text, copied largely from a newsstand astrology column. Lines like "You have a great need for other people to like and admire you" and "At times you have serious doubts as to whether you have made the right decision."

That 87% accuracy rating — which is how the finding is often summarized — became the foundation of what we now call the Barnum effect, after the showman P.T. Barnum's line about something for everyone. The Barnum effect is why fortune cookies work. The messages are engineered to be broad enough that almost anyone can find a shoe that fits. "You will overcome a challenge soon." Well, yeah. You will. Everyone will.

Now here's where it gets interesting for the bad-fortune question. In 2011, psychologists Jane Risen and Thomas Gilovich published work in *Psychological Science* on how people remember predictions. Their finding, distilled: we vividly recall the predictions that come true and quietly forget the ones that don't. If a fortune cookie tells you "Trouble in the workplace this week" and Wednesday brings a tense meeting with your manager, you will remember that fortune forever. If nothing happens, you'll forget the fortune by Friday. Multiply this across a lifetime of cookies and you build up a personal mythology in which the ominous ones "always" come true — because those are the only ones you can recall.

This is confirmation bias doing what it does. It's not a character flaw. It's just how memory works. But it means the fortune's "accuracy" is being retroactively constructed by your own brain.

Then there's the mechanism that concerns me most, and the one worth taking seriously. In 1948 — the same year as Forer's experiment, oddly — the sociologist Robert K. Merton coined the phrase "self-fulfilling prophecy." Merton was writing about bank runs and racial prejudice, but the mechanism is the same one at work with a bad fortune. If you believe something bad is coming, you behave differently. You're more guarded, more suspicious, more prone to read neutral events as hostile. Your friend seems a little short on the phone; normally you'd shrug it off, but today you're primed, so you push back a little, and now the conversation actually does go sideways. The fortune "came true," but only because it changed your inputs.

Ellen Langer's work at Harvard in the 1970s illuminated a related piece of this. Langer ran a series of experiments on what she called the illusion of control — people's tendency to believe they can influence random outcomes, like lottery draws or coin flips. The mirror image of that illusion is the assumption that random outcomes are influencing *you*. When you read "Be cautious in your dealings this week," you slot yourself into a story where the paper knows something you don't. That story feels like insight. It's actually a category error. The paper knows nothing. But the sense that it does can quietly shape your choices for days.

And yet — and this is the part I don't want to skip — belief in fortunes isn't purely a bug. A 2010 study by Damisch, Stoberock, and Mussweiler, also in *Psychological Science*, ran participants through memory and motor tasks after activating a good-luck belief (letting them use "their" lucky ball on a golf putting task, for instance). The lucky group performed measurably better. Belief did real work in the world, through confidence and persistence. In 2003, research by Matthew Hutson found that 72% of college students engaged in some form of magical thinking, even ones who intellectually rejected superstition. This isn't stupidity. It's a feature of how humans navigate uncertainty.

The upshot: belief in fortunes is a real force, but it's a knife that cuts both ways. Positive belief can genuinely lift performance. Negative belief can genuinely tank it. The paper is inert. The reader is not.

## A quick historical detour, because it matters

The whole apparatus of the "Chinese" fortune cookie is itself a manufactured tradition, which puts the whole enterprise in useful perspective. The cookies are not Chinese. They emerged in California in the early 1900s, with competing origin stories: Makoto Hagiwara, the Japanese immigrant caretaker of the Japanese Tea Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park, is credited with serving them to visitors around 1914. David Jung, a Chinese immigrant who founded the Hong Kong Noodle Company in Los Angeles, claimed he invented them around 1918 to hand out to homeless men on the street with inspirational Bible verses inside.

During World War II, Japanese Americans were forcibly interned, and Chinese-owned restaurants filled the fortune-cookie gap. By the late 1940s, most Americans assumed the cookie had always been Chinese. So the entire mystique — the "ancient wisdom" association, the sense that the message is coming from somewhere older and wiser — is a 20th-century American invention overlaid on a Japanese-then-Chinese-American business practice. The fortunes themselves are written by copywriters. Wonton Food in Brooklyn, the largest producer, churns out millions of cookies daily, with fortunes drafted and edited by a small in-house team.

Knowing this doesn't make the ritual less fun. It just makes clear what the ritual actually is: a small, structured moment of attention that lets us tell ourselves a story. Which, honestly, is most of what meaning-making has ever been.

## The honest conclusion

So do bad fortunes come true? Not because the paper is prophetic. But sometimes, yes — because you remember them harder, weigh them heavier, and let them shift your behavior in ways that pull the predicted outcome closer. That's not magic. It's Merton and Kahneman and Risen and Gilovich, all working through your nervous system in real time.

The practical move, if you crack open a slip and read something ominous, is not to force yourself to "not believe it." That rarely works. The move is to notice what your brain is doing: filing this away, priming for evidence, ready to remember the hits and forget the misses. Name the mechanism, and it loses some of its grip. Then, if you want, [break another cookie](/) — or check your [daily fortune](/daily) tomorrow — and let a better message write over it. The ritual is yours. The interpretation is yours. The paper never had a vote to begin with.
