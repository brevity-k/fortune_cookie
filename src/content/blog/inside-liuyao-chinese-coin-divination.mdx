Three brass coins, worn smooth from a century of fingertips, rest in the cupped palm of a man seated at a low wooden table in a side alley off Taipei's Dihua Street. The coins are old — Qing dynasty, probably, with the reign title raised on one side and four characters on the reverse — and the square holes in their centers catch the afternoon light. He shakes them softly, cups them tighter, closes his eyes. Somewhere outside, a scooter buzzes past a dried-mushroom shop. Inside, the air smells of sandalwood and the faint medicinal tang of dried licorice root drifting in from the herbalist next door. He asks a question — silently, the way his teacher taught him — and lets the coins fall.

They land with a soft metallic clatter on the lacquered wood. Two faces up, one down. He makes a mark on a narrow strip of yellow paper, a single unbroken line, and gathers the coins again. He will do this six times. Six tosses, six lines, built from the ground upward like a tiny wooden house, until a complete hexagram sits on the page in front of him — one of exactly 64 possibilities, drawn from a book older than almost any other book still consulted for guidance. This is liuyao. And the man at the table is doing what Chinese diviners have done, in some form, for roughly 3,000 years.

## The Book Behind the Coins

To understand liuyao divination, you have to understand the strange, patient object at its center: the I Ching, or Yijing, the Book of Changes.

Its origins trace to the Western Zhou Dynasty, between 1046 and 771 BCE, though parts of the oracular material likely predate that. Tradition credits King Wen of Zhou, imprisoned by a rival around 1150 BCE, with arranging the 64 hexagrams into the sequence still used today. The story goes that he contemplated the symbols in his cell, pairing them, ordering them, writing short judgments beside each one. His son, the Duke of Zhou, is said to have added commentary on the individual lines. Confucius, centuries later, supposedly spent so much time studying the book that the leather thongs binding his bamboo-strip copy wore out three times.

Whether every detail of that history is literally true matters less than this: by the time the Han dynasty rolled around, the I Ching was already ancient, already revered, and already being consulted by farmers, generals, emperors, and philosophers for help making decisions.

The book itself is simple in structure. Sixty-four hexagrams, each made of six stacked lines, each line either broken (yin) or unbroken (yang). That's it. Each hexagram has a name — The Creative, The Receptive, Difficulty at the Beginning, The Cauldron, Fellowship with Men — and each comes with a short judgment and a set of line statements. When you factor in "changing lines" (we'll get there), the system expands to 4,096 possible readings. Enough variety for almost any question a human could reasonably ask.

## How the Coins Actually Work

The method most people use today is called the three-coin method, and it took hold during the Han dynasty as a faster alternative to the older yarrow stalk ritual, which required 50 dried stalks and nearly an hour of careful sorting. The coin version takes about ten minutes.

You need three coins. Traditionally they're round Chinese cash coins with that distinctive square hole in the middle — the hole exists so coins could be strung together on a cord, but it also neatly expresses the ancient cosmology of a round heaven over a square earth. Brass or bronze, smooth from use, with the reign title of a specific emperor stamped on one face.

Here's how the scoring works, and it's worth getting right because it determines everything that follows. The side with the reign title (usually four characters) is considered yang — heads — and worth 3 points. The reverse, with two or four characters, is yin — tails — worth 2 points. You toss all three coins at once and add up the total:

- Three heads: 9 (old yang, a solid line that is changing into a broken line)
- Three tails: 6 (old yin, a broken line that is changing into a solid line)
- Two heads, one tail: 7 (young yang, a stable solid line)
- Two tails, one head: 8 (young yin, a stable broken line)

You toss six times. The first toss gives you the bottom line of your hexagram. The sixth toss gives you the top. You build upward — this matters, because the hexagram represents a situation growing, developing, moving from root to crown.

If any of your lines are "old" (a 6 or a 9), they're changing. You read your original hexagram first, then flip the changing lines to generate a second hexagram — a picture of where the situation is heading. This is why the book is called the Book of Changes. It doesn't give you a static snapshot. It gives you a before and an after, a sense of motion.

The ritual, done properly, is quiet and attentive. You wash your hands. You sit facing south if you can. You hold the coins between your palms and think about your question — not vaguely, but specifically. "Should I take this job?" is better than "What about my career?" You shake, you release, you record. Six tosses. Six brief suspended moments where the coins spin in the air and your pulse catches, just a little, before they clatter down.

## Jung, Wilhelm, and the Hexagram That Introduced Itself

The I Ching crossed into the Western world slowly. The first full translation was done into Latin by a Jesuit missionary named Jean-Baptiste Régis, published posthumously in 1834. Scholarly, careful, almost no one read it.

The real breakthrough came in 1923, when the German sinologist Richard Wilhelm published his translation into German. Wilhelm had spent more than two decades in Qingdao, where his teacher — a Confucian scholar named Lao Nai-hsüan — walked him line by line through the text. Wilhelm didn't just translate the words; he tried to translate the sensibility, the sense that the book was alive.

Then came Carl Jung. In 1949, Jung wrote the foreword to the English translation of Wilhelm's version, and this is where the story gets wonderful. Jung, uncertain how to introduce such a strange book to a Western audience, decided to simply ask the book itself what it thought of being presented to Westerners. He tossed the coins. He got Hexagram 50 — The Cauldron, Ding — which describes a sacred vessel used for cooking offerings, a container that holds and transforms spiritual nourishment.

Jung took this seriously. He wrote the foreword around it. The experience was part of what led him to formulate his concept of synchronicity — the idea that meaningful coincidences aren't just coincidences, that the inner psyche and outer events sometimes rhyme in ways cause-and-effect can't fully explain. Whether you buy that framework or not, the moment is striking: one of the twentieth century's most famous psychologists sitting at a desk in Küsnacht, Switzerland, three coins in his hand, asking a 3,000-year-old book what it thought of him.

## What a Reading Feels Like

Suppose you throw and get Hexagram 50 yourself. You open the book. You read: "The Cauldron. Supreme good fortune. Success." Then you read the Image: "Fire over wood: the image of the Cauldron. Thus the superior man consolidates his fate by making his position correct."

What does that mean for your question about whether to leave your apartment, confront your sister, apply to graduate school?

The answer, which practitioners have been giving for three millennia, is this: the book doesn't tell you. The book gives you an image, a situation, a pattern. You bring the question. You bring the context. You sit with the hexagram and let it do its slow work, which is less like receiving an answer and more like turning a stone over in your hand until you notice the shape you were already holding.

This is the quiet genius of liuyao. The coins don't predict the future. They interrupt the churn of your own mind long enough for a different kind of thinking to surface. You frame the question carefully. You slow down. You accept an image you didn't choose. You consider it honestly. That process — not the metaphysics — is where the practice has always drawn its power.

## Why People Still Reach for the Coins

Life moves fast and most of our decisions get made in the thin margin between one obligation and the next. We scroll, we scan, we react. Liuyao is slow on purpose. The ritual of washing your hands, holding the coins, asking the question — it forces a pause most of us don't give ourselves otherwise.

There's a reason the practice has survived through dynasties, invasions, cultural revolutions, and digital upheavals. It meets a need that doesn't go away: the need to externalize a question, to give it shape, to hear it reflected back from somewhere outside your own repeating thoughts. Whether you believe the coins carry some whisper of synchronicity or you believe they're simply a beautiful way to focus attention, the result in the moment is the same. You stop. You listen. You consider.

If you're drawn to the small ritual of asking a question and receiving an image back, you might enjoy the simpler version of the same impulse — [break a fortune cookie](/) and see what the slip of paper has to say, or pull up your [daily fortune](/daily) for a quieter kind of reflection. Different tradition, same old human instinct.

## The Table in Taipei, Again

Back in the alley off Dihua Street, the man finishes his sixth toss. Three tails. A six — old yin, a changing line. He writes it carefully at the top of his page, completing the hexagram. He studies it for a long moment, the sandalwood smoke curling up past his shoulder, the scooter long gone, the brass coins warm in his palm from the heat of his hand. He doesn't look surprised. He doesn't look relieved. He looks like someone who has just been handed something to think about — which, after 3,000 years of this exact gesture performed by millions of hands, is really the only thing the coins have ever promised to do.
